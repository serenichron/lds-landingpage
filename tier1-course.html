7 Hidden Pitfalls That Derail Online Learning Projects in Higher Education

By Andrew Doig  ·  Founder, Learning Design Solutions  ·  30 years in HE  ·  7 lessons  ·  ~25–30 minutes

What you'll learn in this course

  • Why recording lectures and uploading them is not online learning
  • How much design work online programmes actually require
  • Why overloading internal teams leads to burnout and poor results
  • How to maintain your institutional identity in the online environment
  • Why pedagogy must always drive technology — not the other way around
  • Why good projects die in committee — and how to build internal buy-in
  • Why cost-per-module is the wrong metric — and what to measure instead

Every year, universities and professional bodies invest significant resources into taking their programmes online. And every year, many of these projects fall short — not because of technology failures, but because of fundamental misunderstandings about what online learning actually requires.

In this course, we'll explore seven specific pitfalls we see repeatedly when institutions start this journey without the right expertise alongside them. The first two lessons are available immediately. Lessons 3–7 explore the deeper challenges and ask for a few details before unlocking.

After 30 years in higher education and having led academic transformation projects for institutions like ICAS, Dyson Institute, and Walbrook, these are the pitfalls we see most often. Understanding them is the first step to avoiding them.

7 Hidden Pitfalls / Pitfall 1

Lesson 1 of 7

Recording lectures and calling it online learning

Pitfall 1: Recording lectures — video lesson by Andrew Doig

Coming soon

Video lesson

In this lesson, we'll explore the most common mistake we see when institutions decide to 'go online': recording existing lectures and uploading them to the VLE. The assumption is that what works in a lecture theatre will work on a screen. It doesn't.

Online learning is a fundamentally different pedagogical experience. Students aren't sitting in a room with social cues, peer pressure to stay engaged, and a lecturer reading the room. They're alone, often distracted, and need entirely different kinds of engagement — structured activities, purposeful interactions, and content designed specifically for the medium.

What we're describing here isn't digital transformation. It's academic transformation — rethinking how teaching and learning work when the classroom disappears. That's a far more significant undertaking than most institutions initially realise.

"Taking courses online isn't a technology project. It's a teaching project."

Next, we'll look at what that teaching project actually involves in terms of design effort — because the gap between expectations and reality here is often the largest single shock for programme teams.

Key takeaways

  • Recorded lectures are a starting point, not a finished online course
  • Online learners need structured activities, interactions, and purpose-built content
  • Going online requires academic transformation, not just technology
  • The pedagogical design is what determines whether students engage and complete

7 Hidden Pitfalls / Pitfall 2

Lesson 2 of 7

Underestimating the design work by a factor of ten

Pitfall 2: Underestimating the expertise required — video lesson by Andrew Doig

Coming soon

Video lesson

In this lesson, we'll explore the second major pitfall: dramatically underestimating how much work building online courses actually involves. Most institutions budget for 'some content development' when what they actually need is a complete course architecture.

That architecture includes: learning outcomes mapped to activities, assessments aligned to outcomes, multimedia production, accessibility compliance, platform configuration, and quality assurance testing. The design work for a single online module typically requires 3–5 times the effort of preparing the equivalent face-to-face teaching.

For a full programme, multiply that across every module. The numbers quickly become significant — and they're numbers that catch most institutions off guard when they first see a realistic project plan.

A single online module typically requires 3–5 times the effort of preparing equivalent face-to-face teaching. For a full programme, multiply that across every module.

We've seen projects stall not because of lack of ambition, but because the initial scoping dramatically underestimated what 'building an online course' actually involves. Getting the scope right at the outset — with realistic timelines and resource commitments — is one of the highest-value things you can do before committing to a programme.

Next, we'll look at what happens when institutions try to absorb this effort into teams that are already running at capacity.

Key takeaways

  • A complete online course requires full course architecture, not just content
  • Expect 3–5x more design effort than equivalent face-to-face teaching
  • Scope and resource planning at the outset prevents costly mid-project stalls
  • Underscoping is the single most common cause of quality compromise

7 Hidden Pitfalls / Pitfall 3

Lesson 3 of 7

Overloading internal teams who are already stretched

Pitfall 3: Overloading internal teams — video lesson by Andrew Doig

Coming soon

Video lesson

In this lesson, we'll explore a pitfall that's often invisible until it's too late: asking existing teams to absorb a major online programme development project on top of everything else they're already doing.

Your internal learning design team — if you have one — is already supporting the entire institution. Asking them to also lead a major online programme development, on top of their existing responsibilities, leads to one outcome: burnout, missed deadlines, and compromised quality.

The capacity problem isn't about capability. Your team may be excellent. It's about bandwidth. Large-scale academic transformation requires dedicated, focused resource that most institutions simply cannot free up internally without sacrificing something else.

The question isn't "can our team do this?" — it's "can our team do this AND everything else they're already responsible for?"

We'll also look at how this connects to quality. When teams are stretched, quality assurance is the first thing that gets cut — and that's precisely when the problems we covered in Lessons 1 and 2 compound. Next, we'll look at a subtler pitfall: what happens to your institutional identity when courses are built under pressure.

Key takeaways

  • Internal team capability is not the issue — available capacity is
  • Overloaded teams produce burnout, delays, and quality shortcuts
  • Quality assurance is the first thing to fall when teams are stretched
  • Dedicated resource for programme development protects both quality and your team's wellbeing

7 Hidden Pitfalls / Pitfall 4

Lesson 4 of 7

Losing your institutional identity in the online version

Pitfall 4: Losing your institutional identity — video lesson by Andrew Doig

Coming soon

Video lesson

In this lesson, we'll explore a pitfall that becomes visible only after the course is live: the loss of the institutional identity that makes your programmes distinctive. This is one of the most underappreciated risks in the entire process.

Your institution has a teaching philosophy, an academic culture, a distinct identity that students and the market recognise. When courses go online through generic templates or are outsourced to vendors who don't understand academia, that identity gets lost.

The online version of your programme should feel unmistakably yours. Your content. Your academic voice. Your intellectual property. Your pedagogical approach. Not a white-label product that could belong to any institution.

"Your content. Your identity. Your IP."

When institutions work with vendors who treat online learning as a production process rather than an academic one, the result is often technically competent but institutionally anonymous. Students notice. And in a competitive market for online learners, that distinction matters enormously.

Next, we'll look at the fifth pitfall — one that's deceptively easy to fall into even when you've done everything else well.

Key takeaways

  • Your institutional identity is a competitive advantage — protect it online
  • Generic templates and outsourced production erase what makes you distinctive
  • Online courses should reflect your academic voice, values, and intellectual property
  • Students and the market recognise authenticity — and its absence

7 Hidden Pitfalls / Pitfall 5

Lesson 5 of 7

Building for a platform instead of building for learners

Pitfall 5: Platform vs learners — video lesson by Andrew Doig

Coming soon

Video lesson

In this lesson, we'll explore the pitfall that's easiest to fall into even when you've navigated the first four successfully: making technology decisions first and fitting the learning design around the platform's capabilities.

We see institutions choose or upgrade their VLE and then try to fit the learning design around what the platform supports. This is backwards. The pedagogy should drive the technology, not the other way around.

A well-designed online course can work on virtually any modern VLE. The platform is a delivery mechanism, not the product. When you start with "what does Moodle support?" instead of "what do our learners need?", you end up with courses that are technically functional but pedagogically mediocre.

Pedagogy first. Platform second. Always.

This principle holds across every decision point in the course design process — from content structure and activity design, through to assessment and quality assurance. When you start with learner needs and let those drive your technology choices, you end up with courses that actually work.

That's the core insight across the first five lessons. In the next two, we'll go deeper on the organisational and strategic challenges that cause even well-designed projects to stall.

Key takeaways

  • Pedagogy must drive technology decisions — not the other way around
  • Start with "what do our learners need?", not "what does our platform support?"
  • A well-designed course works on any modern VLE — the platform is the delivery vehicle
  • Technology-first thinking produces courses that are functional but pedagogically weak

7 Hidden Pitfalls / Pitfall 6

Lesson 6 of 7

The champion problem — why good projects die in committee

The champion problem — video lesson by Andrew Doig

Coming soon

Video lesson

In this lesson, we'll explore one of the most common — and least discussed — reasons online learning projects fail: they never get past the committee stage.

Most institutions have enthusiastic individuals who see the potential of online programme development. A programme director who's seen what competitors are doing. A head of digital education who knows the current approach isn't sustainable. But enthusiasm alone doesn't unlock institutional budgets. These champions need to navigate governance structures, convince risk-averse finance committees, and compete with capital projects that feel more tangible than digital transformation.

The gap between operational enthusiasm and leadership buy-in is where most projects stall. The champion understands the pedagogical opportunity but can't articulate it in the language of institutional strategy — return on investment, competitive positioning, student recruitment, regulatory compliance. Without a compelling business case that speaks to what keeps a vice-chancellor up at night, even the best ideas get deferred to "next year's planning cycle."

What makes the difference is equipping your internal champion with the right tools: a structured readiness assessment they can present to senior leadership, concrete evidence from comparable institutions, and a clear articulation of what the institution gains — and what it risks by waiting. The institutions that move fastest are the ones where someone took the time to build that internal case properly.

The average university committee cycle takes 4–6 months. If your internal champion doesn't have a compelling, data-backed case ready for the next meeting, you've lost half a year.

Key takeaways

  • Internal champions need more than enthusiasm — they need structured evidence and institutional-language arguments
  • The gap between operational excitement and governance approval is the single biggest delay in online programme launches
  • A formal readiness assessment gives champions a presentable, professional deliverable for leadership conversations
  • Institutions that succeed are those that invest in building the internal business case before requesting budget

Lesson 7 of 7

Why cost-per-module is the wrong metric — and what to measure instead

The wrong metric — video lesson by Andrew Doig

Coming soon

Video lesson

In this final lesson, we'll challenge one of the most persistent — and damaging — assumptions in online programme development: that the primary measure of success is cost per module.

When institutions evaluate external partners or internal capacity for online programme development, the conversation almost always starts with cost. How much per module? How does that compare to the agency down the road? This framing creates a race to the bottom where the cheapest option wins — and quality, student outcomes, and institutional reputation pay the price. The hidden costs of "cheaper" approaches are substantial: extensive rework when courses fail quality review, poor student retention that damages programme viability, and reputational damage that takes years to recover from.

The institutions getting the best results are measuring entirely different things: time from concept to launch, student completion rates, learner satisfaction scores, faculty adoption and confidence, and long-term programme sustainability. These are the metrics that determine whether an online programme actually succeeds — whether it recruits students, retains them, and builds the institution's reputation in a competitive market.

Recent advances in AI-enhanced learning design workflows have fundamentally changed the economics. Institutions that partner with specialists who use these approaches achieve faster development timelines and higher quality outputs — not because they've cut corners, but because they've invested in expertise and tooling that eliminates the waste and rework that inflates costs elsewhere. The question isn't "what's the cheapest way to build a module?" — it's "what's the most effective way to build a programme that works?"

Institutions that invest in specialist learning design partnerships report 40–60% faster time-to-launch compared to purely internal builds — with higher student satisfaction scores.

The ICAS qualification rebuild — one of the largest digital transformations in chartered professional education — was delivered on time and won two Learning Technologies Awards. The metric that mattered wasn't cost per module. It was outcomes.

Key takeaways

  • Cost-per-module is a misleading metric that incentivises the wrong decisions
  • Measure what actually determines programme success: completion, satisfaction, time-to-launch, faculty adoption
  • AI-enhanced learning design workflows change the cost equation — faster and higher quality, not cheaper and worse
  • The institutions achieving the best results are those investing in specialist expertise, not shopping for the lowest bid
